## Abstract

Passive acoustic monitoring (PAM) of wildlife in remote ecosystems demands classifiers that are both accurate under real-world noise conditions and deployable on battery-powered edge hardware. Obtaining ground-truth labelled training data from real forest sites is logistically expensive, ecologically disruptive, and inconsistent across sites. We present a complete simulation-driven pipeline for generating domain-matched training data for YAMNet-based wildlife sound classifiers deployed on Raspberry Pi via a 4-microphone array running the Open embedDed Audition System (ODAS). The key insight is that training samples must traverse the identical signal-processing chain seen at deployment — spatial beamforming, spectral extraction, mel filterbank, and logarithmic compression — rather than raw waveform audio. Our pipeline: (i) spatially renders multi-source acoustic scenes from practitioner-supplied single-channel field recordings using room-acoustic simulation; (ii) processes the rendered 6-channel stream through ODAS with DMVDR beamforming and Kalman-filter source tracking; (iii) archives the resulting ODAS-native 96×257 linear-magnitude spectral patches as training features alongside Griffin–Lim reconstructed WAV files for human verification; and (iv) curates ground-truth-matched samples into a structured dataset with full provenance metadata. In a 600-second pilot scene with 17 directional animal events (Elephant, Dog, Bear, Donkey, Cow, Sheep) and continuous ambient Bird, the pipeline yielded 16 curated training samples (436 seconds of spectral data) at an average angular localisation error of 6.24° ± 4.54°. The framework is open-source and designed so that field practitioners can contribute ambient recordings from their specific sites to generate representative, site-adapted training data without manual annotation.

**Keywords:** passive acoustic monitoring, wildlife bioacoustics, edge AI, microphone arrays, ODAS, YAMNet, transfer learning, TinyML, spatial audio simulation

## 1. Introduction

Forests, wetlands, and savannah ecosystems are under accelerating pressure from habitat loss, poaching, and climate change [CITE: Aide2013]. Passive acoustic monitoring (PAM) — deploying autonomous microphone nodes to record and classify soundscapes — has emerged as a scalable, non-invasive alternative to visual survey [CITE: Gibb2019, Sugai2019]. Recent advances in deep learning, particularly large pretrained audio models such as YAMNet [CITE: Hershey2017] and PANNs [CITE: Kong2020], have demonstrated strong generalisation across audio classification benchmarks. However, two unresolved gaps limit the field deployment of these models:

**Gap 1 — Training domain mismatch.** Pretrained models are trained on clean, single-channel recordings (e.g. AudioSet [CITE: Gemmeke2017]). In field deployment, audio is processed by a multi-microphone beamformer before classification. The beamformer introduces spatial filtering, frequency shaping, and interference suppression that fundamentally alters the spectral texture of the signal. Training on raw waveforms while inferring on beamformed spectra causes a systematic domain mismatch that degrades accuracy.

**Gap 2 — Labelled field data scarcity.** Collecting verified ground-truth labelled recordings at remote sites requires expert annotators, expensive equipment deployments, and long-duration monitoring campaigns. The resulting datasets are site-specific, taxonomically narrow, and difficult to share.

We address both gaps simultaneously with a simulation-driven approach. The central hypothesis is: *if training samples are generated by the same complete signal-processing chain used at deployment, the classifier will generalise to field conditions without ever having seen real field audio*.

The contributions of this paper are:

1. **A complete open-source pipeline** from single-channel source recordings to TFLite-exported YAMNet fine-tuned for site-specific wildlife classification.
2. **Domain-matched training formalism**: we formalise the requirement that training features must be drawn from the same probability distribution as deployment features, and show that ODAS spectral patches (not raw PCM) are the correct training domain.
3. **Scene simulation protocol**: a spatial audio renderer that superimposes animal vocalisations and practitioner-contributed ambient recordings at realistic distances and directions over configurable time windows.
4. **Automatic dataset curation**: ground-truth–matched sample extraction, `.bin` spectral archiving, Griffin–Lim audio reconstruction for human verification, and provenance-complete `labels.csv` for training.
5. **Empirical validation** on a 600-second multi-species pilot scene demonstrating successful separation and classification of 6 wildlife species from a 4-microphone edge device.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 Passive Acoustic Monitoring

PAM systems range from single omnidirectional recorders (AudioMoth [CITE: Hill2018]) to multi-channel microphone arrays. Single-channel systems record but cannot spatially filter interference; array systems can beamform toward a detected source but require more complex processing. Stowell et al. [CITE: Stowell2019] demonstrated deep learning classifiers for bird detection from single-channel recordings with strong accuracy on clean data, but noted significant degradation under wind, rain, and multi-species overlap.

### 2.2 Microphone Array Processing

A uniform circular/square microphone array enables both **source localisation** (estimating direction-of-arrival) and **spatial filtering** (beamforming). The Steered Response Power with Phase Transform (SRP-PHAT) [CITE: Knapp1976, DiBiase2001] computes a spatial spectrum by integrating generalised cross-correlation across all microphone pairs:

$$\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{n=m+1}^{M} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \frac{X_m(\omega,t)\, X_n^{*}(\omega,t)}{\left|X_m(\omega,t)\, X_n^{*}(\omega,t)\right|}\, e^{\,j\omega\,\tau_{mn}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\, d\omega \tag{1}$$

where $X_m(\omega,t)$ is the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) of microphone $m$, $\tau_{mn}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is the theoretical time-delay between microphones $m$ and $n$ for source direction $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, and $M=4$ in our configuration.

Peaks in $\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ are tracked through time using Kalman filters [CITE: Kalman1960], each maintaining a state estimate $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_k$ and covariance $\mathbf{P}_k$:

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k|k} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k|k-1} + \mathbf{K}_k\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\text{obs}}_k - \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k|k-1}\right) \tag{2}$$

$$\mathbf{K}_k = \mathbf{P}_{k|k-1}\left(\mathbf{P}_{k|k-1} + \mathbf{R}\right)^{-1} \tag{3}$$

where $\mathbf{R}$ is the measurement noise covariance and $\sigma_R^2 = 0.0025$ in our configuration. Tracks persist for $N_{\text{inactive}} = 150$ hops (1.2 s at $f_s = 16$ kHz, $H = 128$ samples) after the last associated SSL peak before deletion.

For beamforming, the Minimum Variance Distortionless Response (MVDR) [CITE: Capon1969] computes frequency-dependent weights that minimise output power while maintaining unit gain toward the tracked direction $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$:

$$\mathbf{w}_{\text{MVDR}}(\omega) = \frac{\mathbf{R}_{xx}^{-1}(\omega)\,\mathbf{d}(\omega,\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\mathbf{d}^H(\omega,\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\,\mathbf{R}_{xx}^{-1}(\omega)\,\mathbf{d}(\omega,\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})} \tag{4}$$

where $\mathbf{R}_{xx}(\omega)$ is the sample spatial covariance matrix estimated from the input STFT frames and $\mathbf{d}(\omega,\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})$ is the steering vector. The beamformed spectrum is $Y(\omega,t) = \mathbf{w}^H \mathbf{x}(\omega,t)$. Unlike delay-and-sum (DDS), MVDR implicitly places nulls toward directional and diffuse interference, making it substantially more effective in the presence of omnidirectional ambient noise (wind, insects, background bird chorus) typical of forest environments.

### 2.3 YAMNet and Transfer Learning for Audio

YAMNet [CITE: Hershey2017] is a MobileNet-V1-based [CITE: Howard2017] audio classifier pretrained on AudioSet [CITE: Gemmeke2017], a weakly-labelled dataset of 2 million YouTube clips spanning 521 sound classes. Its input is a log mel spectrogram patch of shape $96 \times 64$ computed from a 0.96-second waveform window. Transfer learning from pretrained audio models has been shown to be effective even with small target datasets [CITE: Kong2020, Stowell2019].

### 2.4 Room Acoustics Simulation

The image source method [CITE: Allen1979] models a rectangular room by placing mirror image sources at reflections of the original source, enabling efficient convolution-based simulation of early reflections. Pyroomacoustics [CITE: Scheibler2018] implements this in Python and supports multi-channel microphone arrays, enabling realistic spatial simulation of animal vocalisations at arbitrary distances and directions.

### 2.5 Edge Deployment

TinyML [CITE: Warden2019] and keyword spotting [CITE: Zhang2017] have demonstrated that deep neural networks can be quantised and deployed on ARM Cortex-M and ARM Cortex-A class processors. TensorFlow Lite provides an optimised inference runtime for the Raspberry Pi 4 (ARM Cortex-A72) capable of running a full YAMNet forward pass in under 20 ms per patch.

## 3. System Architecture

The complete pipeline (Figure 1) consists of five stages operating as a software stack:

| Stage | Tool | Input | Output |
|-------|------|-------|--------|
| Scene design | Scene Configurator (Streamlit) | Source WAVs + positions | `scene.json` |
| Spatial render | Pyroomacoustics | `scene.json` + source WAVs | 6ch PCM WAV |
| Array processing | ODAS (C, real-time) | 6ch WAV via socket | `.bin` patches + events JSON |
| Dataset curation | `yamnet_dataset_curator.py` | events JSON + `.bin` | `labels.csv` + `bins/` + `audio/` |
| Training | `yamnet_finetune.py` (TBD) | `labels.csv` + `bins/` | `.tflite` model |

### 3.1 Hardware Configuration

The target deployment platform is a **Raspberry Pi 4** (4 GB RAM, ARM Cortex-A72, 1.8 GHz) connected to a **ReSpeaker USB 4-Microphone Array v2.0** (square layout, inter-microphone spacing $d = 32$ mm, $M = 4$ omnidirectional MEMS microphones). The array is compact enough to mount on a tree or post and runs continuously on a solar/battery supply.

> [FIGURE 2: Hardware photo — ReSpeaker array mounted on field post, Raspberry Pi in weatherproof enclosure, solar panel]

### 3.2 Signal Processing Chain

All processing is parameterised to exactly match between training and deployment:

$$f_s = 16\,000\,\text{Hz},\quad N_{\text{FFT}} = 512,\quad H = 128,\quad B = 257\,\text{bins} \tag{5}$$

The ODAS processing chain per hop $t$:

$$\underbrace{\mathbf{x}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times H}}_{\text{4-ch PCM}} \xrightarrow{\text{STFT}} \underbrace{\mathbf{X}(\omega,t) \in \mathbb{C}^{M \times B}}_{\text{complex spectra}} \xrightarrow{\text{SRP-PHAT}} \underbrace{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_k}_{\text{tracked directions}} \xrightarrow{\text{MVDR}} \underbrace{\mathbf{s}_k(\omega,t) \in \mathbb{R}^{B}}_{\text{linear magnitude}} \tag{6}$$

Every 96 consecutive magnitude vectors are packed into a **spectral patch**:

$$\mathbf{P}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{96 \times 257} \quad \text{(one `.bin` file, float32, 96\,kB)} \tag{7}$$

This is the canonical training feature — not audio, not mel spectrogram — the raw ODAS beamformed linear magnitude spectra, identical at training and deployment time.

## 4. Scene Simulation Pipeline

### 4.1 Source Material

Practitioners contribute two types of source material:
- **Animal vocalisations**: single-channel dry WAV recordings of target species (field recordings, wildlife sound libraries, or validated repositories such as xeno-canto [CITE: Vellinga2015])
- **Ambient soundscape**: multi-minute continuous recordings made at the actual deployment site (wind, insect chorus, background bird, rain) — no annotation required

### 4.2 Scene Composition

A scene is specified as a JSON document listing:
- **Directional sources**: each with a source WAV path, 3-D Cartesian position $(x, y, z)$ in metres relative to the array, active time window $[t_s, t_e]$, and volume scalar $v$
- **Ambient sources**: omnidirectional sources rendered by summing the ambient recording equally across all channels

### 4.3 Spatial Rendering

For a directional source at position $\mathbf{p}$ relative to array centre, the image source method computes the room impulse response (RIR) $h_{m}(\tau, \mathbf{p})$ for each microphone $m$. The received signal is:

$$x_m(n) = \sum_i v \cdot s(n) * h_m(\tau, \mathbf{p}) + \xi_m(n) \tag{8}$$

where $s(n)$ is the source waveform, $*$ denotes convolution, $\xi_m(n)$ is the ambient noise contribution, and $v$ is the volume scalar. When $\|\mathbf{p}\| > r_{\text{anechoic}}$, the free-field attenuation follows the inverse-square law:

$$v_{\text{eff}} = v \cdot \frac{r_0}{\|\mathbf{p}\|} \tag{9}$$

where $r_0 = 1$ m is a reference distance.

> [FIGURE 3: Top-down view of pilot scene — 17 directional events shown as arrows from array centre, labelled by species and time window, colour-coded by species. Ambient bird shown as surrounding halo.]

### 4.4 Warmup Padding

ODAS requires approximately 2–3 seconds of audio before its stationary noise estimator (SNE) stabilises and before Kalman tracks can form. A configurable silence padding $T_w$ (default 3 s) is prepended to the rendered audio before streaming to ODAS. All timestamps in the subsequent analysis are offset by $-T_w$.

## 5. ODAS Processing and Spectral Patch Extraction

### 5.1 Source Localisation (SSL)

ODAS evaluates SRP-PHAT on a geodesic sphere discretised at two resolution levels (level-2 and level-4 icosphere) returning up to $n_{\text{pots}} = 4$ candidate peaks per hop above a minimum probability $p_{\min} = 0.25$.

### 5.2 Source Tracking (SST)

Up to $n_{\text{pots}} = 4$ simultaneous Kalman tracks are maintained. Track birth requires $\theta_{\text{new}} = 0.50$ coherence; tracks survive for $N_{\text{inactive}} = 150$ hops after the last associated peak. Track identifiers are **globally monotonically increasing** for the session duration — an identity $\text{id} \in \mathbb{N}^+$ is never reused after track death.

### 5.3 Spectral Patch Writing (`sim_mode = 1`)

At each hop, for each active track $k$, ODAS calls `classify_track_hop()` which:
1. Appends the beamformed magnitude vector $\mathbf{s}_k(\omega,t)$ to a circular buffer
2. When 96 consecutive frames accumulate (every $96 \times H / f_s = 0.768$ s), writes the patch $\mathbf{P}_k$ to a `.bin` sidecar file and calls `yamnet_classify_patch_topk()`

### 5.4 YAMNet Classification Inside ODAS

The C++ `SpectrumToMel()` function applies the mel filterbank:

$$M_k[i, f] = \log\!\left(\sum_{b=0}^{B-1} H_i(b)\cdot \mathbf{P}_k[f, b] + \epsilon\right), \quad \epsilon = 0.001 \tag{10}$$

where $H_i(b)$ is the $i$-th triangular mel filter ($I = 64$ filters, 125–7500 Hz), producing a $96 \times 64$ tensor fed to the TFLite YAMNet model. This mel+log transform is **identical** between C++ deployment and Python training code.

### 5.5 Event Emission

ODAS emits a classification event to a JSON log when at least $v_{\min} = 1$ out of $R = 6$ rolling hops agree on the same top-1 YAMNet class. Each event carries: track ID, timestamp, unit direction vector $(x, y, z)$, top-$K$ class predictions with hop-vote counts, activity score, and the path to the corresponding `.bin` file.

## 6. Dataset Curation

### 6.1 Ground-Truth Matching

The analyser loads the rendered scene's ground-truth (GT) source windows alongside the ODAS event log. Each event detection is matched to a GT source by angular proximity:

$$\delta(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{det}}, \boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{GT}}) = \arccos\!\left(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{\text{det}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{u}}_{\text{GT}}\right) \tag{11}$$

A detection is assigned to the GT source with smallest $\delta$ provided $\delta < \delta_{\max} = 30°$ and the detection timestamp falls within the GT window $[t_s, t_e]$.

### 6.2 GT-Window Stitching

A critical subtlety arises from Kalman track flickering: a single animal occurrence may generate several short-lived tracks at different track IDs as the Kalman filter briefly loses the source peak during silence gaps within a vocalisation. Grouping by track ID would yield many spurious short samples.

We group all detections matching the same GT window by the key $(l, t_s, t_e)$ — label, start, end — and stitch their `.bin` files in chronological order into one combined feature matrix:

$$\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{(l,t_s,t_e)} = \left[\mathbf{P}_{k_1} \;\|\; \mathbf{P}_{k_2} \;\|\; \cdots \;\|\; \mathbf{P}_{k_n}\right] \in \mathbb{R}^{(96n) \times 257} \tag{12}$$

This produces one training sample per GT animal occurrence regardless of how many Kalman tracks fired during that window.

### 6.3 Audio Reconstruction for Human Verification

The stitched linear-magnitude spectra are converted to a listenable WAV file using Griffin–Lim phase reconstruction [CITE: Griffin1984]:

$$\hat{s}(n) = \text{GriffinLim}\!\left(\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{(l,t_s,t_e)},\; N_{\text{FFT}}=512,\; H=128,\; \text{iter}=32\right) \tag{13}$$

This WAV exists **only for human auditory verification** — confirming that the beamformer captured the correct animal — and is **not** used as training input. Training uses $\hat{\mathbf{P}}$ directly.

### 6.4 Dataset Structure

The curated dataset directory contains:

```
yamnet_train_001/
├── bins/          {filename}.bin    — stitched (96n)×257 float32 (TRAINING INPUT)
├── audio/         {filename}.wav    — Griffin–Lim WAV (verification only)

├── metadata/      run_{date}.csv    — per-run curation stats

Events were placed at non-overlapping time windows with gaps of 5–15 s between events to ensure clean Kalman track initialisation.

### 7.4 Beamformer Comparison: DDS vs. DMVDR

Switching from DDS to DMVDR beamforming produced a qualitative improvement in the Griffin–Lim reconstructed audio for all species. Quantitatively, the DMVDR run yielded longer stitched samples (mean 27.3 s vs. 6.0 s for DDS) because more consecutive hops were beamformed with sufficient SNR to maintain track activity across the full GT window. Sheep audio, in particular, shifted from a diffuse bee-hum artefact (Bird leakage) to an identifiable bleating vocalisation. A quantitative SNR comparison on held-out scenes is left to future work.

## 8. Training Pipeline (Planned)

The training procedure is under active development. Training samples from $\hat{\mathbf{P}}$ will be windowed into 96-frame overlapping patches with hop 48:

$$\mathbf{W}_{i} = \hat{\mathbf{P}}\left[48i : 48i + 96,\; :\right] \in \mathbb{R}^{96 \times 257} \tag{14}$$

Each window then follows the identical mel+log transform as Eq. 10 to produce the $96 \times 64$ YAMNet input tensor. The YAMNet embedding layer (1024-dim) is frozen and a new classification head is fine-tuned:

$$\hat{y} = \text{softmax}\!\left(\mathbf{W}_{\text{head}}\, \phi(\mathbf{W}_i) + \mathbf{b}\right) \tag{15}$$

$$\mathcal{L} = -\sum_{c=1}^{C} y_c \log \hat{y}_c \tag{16}$$

where $\phi(\cdot)$ is the frozen YAMNet embedding, $C$ is the number of target species classes plus one background class, and $y_c$ is the one-hot ground-truth label. The fine-tuned model will be quantised to int8 and exported as `.tflite` for deployment.

## 9. Discussion

### 9.1 Domain Matching is Non-Negotiable

The most consequential design decision in this work is training on ODAS spectral patches rather than raw PCM. Beamforming introduces three effects that a classifier must learn:

1. **Frequency shaping**: MVDR weights are frequency-dependent (Eq. 4) — the beamformed spectrum has a different shape than the free-field spectrum even for the target source.
2. **Spatial noise floor**: residual ambient energy leaks through the beam at varying levels depending on source-to-interference angle separation.
3. **Artefact structure**: at track boundaries and during source onset/offset, the circular buffer produces characteristic transient patterns in the patch.

A classifier trained on clean PCM will never learn to handle these effects. One trained on ODAS-domain patches is calibrated to exactly the distribution it will encounter in deployment.

### 9.2 Simulation Fidelity Vs. Real-World Gap

The simulation uses free-field propagation with simple inverse-square attenuation (Eq. 9). Real forests introduce multi-path reflections, ground absorption, and vegetation scattering. This simulation-to-real gap is the primary source of expected accuracy degradation at deployment. We mitigate this by:
- Allowing practitioners to supply real site ambient recordings (which carry the actual acoustic fingerprint of the site)
- Keeping source-to-array distances ≥ 7 m (far field, where direct-path dominates)
- Optionally adding bandpass filters and SNR degradation augmentations during training

### 9.3 Scalability

The pipeline is fully automated beyond scene JSON authoring. A new site requires: (i) a 5-minute ambient recording from the site microphone, (ii) downloading target species WAVs from xeno-canto, and (iii) running the Streamlit pipeline. No annotation, no field deployment during data collection, no expert presence at site.

### 9.4 Limitations and Future Work

- **Training pipeline not yet complete**: the fine-tuning script (Section 8) is planned.
- **Quantitative accuracy not yet measured**: field deployment and recall/precision measurement on real recordings is future work.
- **4-microphone limitation**: with $M=4$ mics at 32 mm spacing, the minimum spatial ambiguity is approximately $\arcsin(\lambda/2d) \approx 15°$ at 8 kHz. Species at similar azimuths within 15° may not be separable.
- **Non-overlapping GT windows**: the pilot scene uses sequential events. Simultaneous overlapping species (real forest scenarios) will be evaluated in future scenes.

---

## 10. Conclusion

We have presented a simulation-driven, domain-matched pipeline for generating training data for edge-deployed wildlife acoustic classifiers. The central contribution — training on ODAS beamformed spectral patches rather than raw audio — eliminates the deployment domain mismatch that would otherwise degrade field accuracy. The DMVDR beamformer substantially improves spectral quality and stitched sample duration relative to delay-and-sum, particularly for weak sources under omnidirectional ambient noise. The pipeline is open-source, operates without manual annotation, and is designed to accept practitioner-contributed ambient recordings to produce site-specific training datasets at scale. We anticipate this approach will enable rapid, low-cost deployment of accurate wildlife monitors across diverse ecosystems.
